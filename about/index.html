Map Updates

Hi, I'm Mike. I live in Provinceton, which is either in Clayton or Cloverdale depending on who you ask, and the Surrey Langley SkyTrain is going up a short walk from my house. Most days I'm out along the line with Piper and Toby, my two trusty assistants, or on my bike, watching the Clayton Clipper inch its way west one span at a time.

This started as a drawing. I had been staring at the bents and hammers for months, trying to work out what they were going to build. Only a few pieces were up. Patterns in the beam connection points on the supports, seen from the ground and in drone videos, plus the placement of the temporary wooden platforms, let me picture where a crane-lifted girder would go and what the gantry would build. I needed a visual to share my idea on a forum, so in mid-October I sketched the stretch past the South Fraser Maintenance and Storage Facility. It kept growing into a map of the Clayton Clipper's whole route, plus Trackside, where I post what I see along the way.

It is still a prediction.

What is the Clayton Clipper? It's a launching gantry: a giant yellow machine, made by a company in Italy called Deal, that builds the guideway, the concrete bridge SkyTrain will run on, along Fraser Highway. A trolley crane runs along the top of the truss, lifting 12 concrete segments off trucks and hanging them in a row between two support pillars. Workers glue the segments together. Then they pull steel cables through them and tighten the cables until the row is one solid span, about 40 metres long. Next, the gantry walks forward to the next gap, lifting its legs with hydraulic jacks.

What model is it? Deal doesn't publish one, so I asked Claude to dig through Deal's catalogues. It couldn't find an exact model number, but it cracked the naming scheme. Deal gives its gantries codes like LGS-SS-O-340-36, the one that built the Canada Line: it builds one span at a time, hangs the segments from above, lifts 340 tonnes, and reaches 36 metres. The Clayton Clipper is almost certainly from the same LGS-SS-O family. It hangs 12 segments across spans just over 40 metres, more than the Canada Line machine was built for, so Claude's best guess is something like LGS-SS-O-400-40. Its beam-style cousins, the Langley Launcher and Fleetwood Flyer, are probably bigger: the span across 184 St measures about 44 metres on aerial photos.

Why only the Clayton Clipper? There are four gantries building this line. The Fleetwood Flyer is done, and as of Nov 1, 2026, the Surrey Sprinter only has about six spans to go. The Langley Launcher is just too far to walk. So I'm sticking with the Clayton Clipper. But the whole thing is open source, so if someone wants to take on the Langley Launcher, go for it.

It's just me. This is a hobby project. I'm not connected to TransLink, TI Corp, the Province, or anyone building the line, and nobody official has checked any of this. Spans and supports are placed as carefully as I can manage, but they're approximate. Anything that hasn't been built yet is my best guess, so please don't plan anything important around it.

How it's made. The first version was a single, very large HTML file that the Claude app built as a viewer for my drawing. To split it into manageable pieces, I moved it to Svelte, which looked simpler than React, and I got to learn a new framework along the way. It's hosted on GitHub Pages. I update it from the Claude app while I'm out on the bike or walking the dogs, and do the bigger changes with Claude Code back at my computer. All the code is on GitHub.

Why Svelte? The map is one big SVG drawing, and the code needs to reach inside it to colour individual spans, place the gantry, and switch layers on and off. Showing it with an ordinary <img> tag would have turned it into a black box. Svelte made it easy to split the code into components while keeping the SVG embedded directly in the page, and its reactivity means flipping a layer switch updates the map automatically. It also builds Trackside into plain HTML pages, so search engines can read them, and it compiles down to small, plain JavaScript. For a static site on GitHub Pages, it turned out to be a good balance of organization, flexibility and simplicity.
